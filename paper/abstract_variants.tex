\noindent
A greenbeard is a signal that lets its bearers treat one another differently
from everyone else, and its classical vulnerability is that an impostor can wear
it. Machine agents are beginning to carry exactly such signals: provenance
attestations and certification marks, cheap to check and possible to forge. We
make the mark a strategic variable in a reduced four-strategy model of a
competitive development race, holding the underlying game fixed so that every
effect belongs to the identity layer. A design is a triple: a badge that is
genuine, forged or absent, the conduct it runs towards partners whose badge
passes verification, and the conduct it runs towards everyone else. A forged
badge passes with probability $\sigma$. Identity matters exactly where
deterrence fails: below a reciprocity threshold no unbadged population sustains
safe play. A club that merely reciprocates within its membership tolerates more
than twice the forgery of one that trusts its members unconditionally, so
in-group conduct, not goodwill, sets how much impersonation a mark survives.
Fines cannot substitute for detection, because the protective fine diverges as
forgery becomes undetectable. Collapse is graceful in the wrong way: a mimic
that forges the badge and behaves well invades before the exploiter does,
hollowing out the credential while conduct still looks sound, and assortment
leaves this as the only observed failure mode among the sampled transversal
invaders. The flow is multistable, with two dominant safe faces and a targeted,
numerically robust unsafe badge-mixed rest face outside the uniform sample, so
certification buys robustness rather than a lower level of harm,
and what holds it up is the rent it charges outsiders.

\noindent
Agents in a machine economy increasingly carry an identity: a provenance
attestation, a certification mark, or simply a recognisable house style. Such a
mark is a \emph{greenbeard} in the classical sense, a signal that lets its
bearers treat one another differently from everyone else, with the classical
vulnerability that it can be worn by an impostor. We make the mark a strategic
variable in the reduced four-strategy AI race of Fern\'andez Domingos and Han,
which we use unmodified so that every effect we report belongs to the identity
layer. A design is a triple: a badge that is genuine, forged or absent, the
conduct it runs towards partners whose badge passes its check, and the conduct
it runs towards everyone else. A forged badge passes with probability $\sigma$,
so $1 - \sigma$ is the detection power of the verification technology.
Eight results follow. First, identity matters exactly where liability does not:
below the reciprocity threshold $L_R = 0.551$ of this race, no unbadged
population can hold safe play at all, because retaliation in kind cannot claw
back a stolen opening move before the horizon. Second, a certified club that
merely reciprocates inside its membership tolerates more than twice the forgery
of a club that trusts unconditionally, $\sigma^{*} = 0.866$ against $0.400$:
in-group conduct is not a matter of goodwill but of how much impersonation the
mark can survive. Third, fines cannot substitute for detection. The protective
fine diverges as $\sigma \to 1$, rising from $11.9$ at $\sigma = 0.90$ to
$428.6$ at $\sigma = 0.99$, which is the Becker trade-off with the detection
probability set by cryptography rather than by policing effort. Fourth, no
badge nucleates a club from anarchy on its own: at zero assortment a genuine
badge is a pure cost, and clubs require $r > r^{*} = 0.063$ whatever the
verification quality in that invasion problem.
Fifth, collapse is graceful in the wrong way. The behavioural mimic invades at
$\sigma_m = 0.938$, below the exploiter's threshold, so attestation is hollowed
out while conduct still looks fine: at $\sigma = 0.97$ forgers hold $55\%$ of
the population and behave impeccably, the unsafe frequency has if anything
improved to $0.058$, and the posterior that a passed badge is genuine has
fallen to $0.05$. Worse, clustering makes this the only observed failure mode
left among the sampled invaders. It
raises the exploiter's threshold from $0.866$ to $1$ by $r = 0.134$ and moves
the mimic's by $0.001$, so the instrument that makes clubs possible also
concentrates all residual risk in the failure that conduct monitoring cannot
see. Sixth, trust does not rebuild: at zero assortment
a collapsed population admits no re-entry at any verification quality.
Seventh, provider-scoped marks make the ecosystem unsafe frequency exactly
$1 - \mathrm{HHI}$, so a fragmented market is an unsafe one unless marks are
federated, and the
exclusion rent falls from $30.4$ under a monopoly to $2.5$ across eight
providers. Eighth, and the reason the first seven do not add up to an
endorsement: what certification buys a settled market is an entry barrier, not
safety. The replicator flow is multistable, with two dominant safe faces; all
$20\,000$ uniform interior starts reached a regime that is either entirely
badged ($38.8\%$) or entirely unbadged, and both are internally safe. A
targeted, numerically robust unsafe mixed rest face exists outside that sample.
The certified regime is
therefore poorer than the uncertified one by exactly its dues, $57.0$ against
$59.0$; certification lowers harm only in the small-mutation reading, where it
protects a safe market from being knocked out of safety rather than making a
settled one safer. The exclusion is paid at the boundary: a compliant unbadged
outsider playing the club's own conduct earns $26.64$ against a member's
$57.00$, a toll of $30.36$ for no conduct-related reason. A club charging no
toll has spoof threshold zero and never survives, and fining forgery restores
its threshold to $0.923$ but leaves its share below $0.032$. The exclusion is
not a side effect of the mark; it is what holds the mark up. What the fine does
buy is a meaningful credential, raising attestation integrity from $0.16$ to
$0.98$, which is the instrument we end up recommending alongside federation.
